# Photolithography

Photolithography transfers a pattern from a photomask onto a substrate coated with a light-sensitive polymer called photoresist. It is the step that defines feature shapes in semiconductor manufacturing.

## Process steps

A typical flow is: clean the wafer, perform a dehydration bake, apply an adhesion promoter such as HMDS, spin-coat the photoresist, soft-bake it to drive out solvent, expose it to ultraviolet light through the mask, perform a post-exposure bake if the resist requires one, develop the resist to remove the exposed or unexposed regions, and finally hard-bake the remaining pattern before etching or deposition.

## Positive and negative resist

In a positive resist the exposed regions become more soluble and are removed by the developer, so the pattern on the wafer matches the opaque areas of the mask. In a negative resist the exposed regions become less soluble and remain after developing.

## Image reversal and lift-off

Image reversal resists can be processed to behave as either positive or negative resist. In image reversal mode, a reversal bake followed by a flood exposure produces an undercut sidewall profile. The undercut breaks the continuity of metal deposited over the resist, which makes metal lift-off clean: when the resist is dissolved, the metal on top of it lifts away and only the metal deposited directly on the substrate remains.

## Resolution

The minimum printable feature size is described by the Rayleigh criterion, CD = k1 * lambda / NA, where lambda is the exposure wavelength, NA is the numerical aperture of the optics and k1 is a process-dependent factor. Shorter wavelengths and larger numerical apertures print smaller features.

## Cleanrooms

Lithography is done in cleanrooms because particles cause defects. Under the older US FED-STD-209E classification, a Class 100 cleanroom allows at most 100 particles of 0.5 micrometres or larger per cubic foot of air. Lithography areas use yellow light because the resists are sensitive to ultraviolet and blue light.
